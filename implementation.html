Implementing Those Ideas Into Your Portfolio

Now, let’s apply this knowledge practically to construct the ideal portfolio. The ideal portfolio should contain only stocks which are discounted by market risk, diversified as well as possible. But all stocks are exposed to market risk, because market risk is the risk you take for investing in the market at all, so all stocks are discounted by it[6].

Thus, a portfolio of all the stocks in the world will be the best-diversified portfolio to capture market risk – and VT (the ticker symbol for the Vanguard Total World Stock Fund) is that portfolio. VT bundles all the world’s stocks together into a single convenient low-cost fund. Worldwide diversification in particular is important for any rational portfolio – Japan was once the world’s second largest and fastest growing economy, and it suffered a crash 30 years ago that it still hasn’t recovered from. Even though a single country may never recover from a crash, the world as a whole will.

As long as your investments are diversified, the most threatening risk to your profits is your own psychological tolerance to short-term volatility. Investors often panic-sell during crashes and this can cripple long-term goals. If you want to reduce psychological risk, and suspect you might panic if the market crashes, you can reduce volatility by adding BNDW, an index of all the world’s bonds. Use a resource like this to help you decide if you’d like to reduce psychological risk, and by how much.

When a company decides to lower its stock value and give you that lowered value as cash, that is called a dividend. Ensure you automatically reinvest dividends for all purchased funds, or else they will become uninvested over time. And expense ratios compound, so they must be kept as low as possible. A 1% expense ratio means after 40 years you will only have (99%)40 = 0.9940 = 67% of your money, meaning the expense cost you a third of your net worth, while a 0.06% expense ratio will leave you with 0.999440 = 97.6% after 40 years.

As long as you have taxable income during a year, you can contribute that income to a Roth or Trad IRA and a Roth or Trad 401K for retirement and get a tax advantage. Roth contributions are taxed as income the year you make them, whereas Trad contributions are taxed as income during retirement, so contribute Roth whenever your current income is less than your expected income during retirement and you should contribute Trad when your current income is more than your retirement income. Withdrawing money from your Trad IRA/401K and standard investment accounts does count as income, while Roth withdrawals do not count. If your tax bracket is the same during retirement and employment, it does not matter which one you choose because of the commutativity of multiplication. Imagine your money doubles between now and retirement and you have a 20% tax rate now and in retirement. Then, you will have

(20% lost from tax) 0.8 * 2 = 2 * 0.8 = 1.6

regardless of whether you pay your taxes now (Roth) or later (Trad). So if your tax rate now is the same as during retirement, it doesn’t matter if you pay taxes now (Roth) or later (Trad).

For most people, investing has a winning strategy. Buy VT and contribute to a Roth/Trad IRA & 401K. Do not sell until retirement, especially in response to underperformance. Find an appropriate psychological risk vs. return tradeoff, choose to contribute Roth vs. Trad based on your current tax bracket, and ensure you reinvest dividends automatically.

Appendix: Roth/Trad Cheat Sheet

You should plan to have at least $500k in Trad contributions by the time you retire (in 2026 dollars, or its inflation-equivalent). This is because of the standard deduction, in 2026 the first ~$16K of income you make is taxed at 0% and you subtract that amount from your taxable income. So if you live 30 years into retirement, your first $16k of Trad withdrawals per year is totally tax free. Similarly, if you are making less than $16k before retirement, you should always make Roth contributions.
